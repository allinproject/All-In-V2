# V2 Exercise Science Evidence Baseline — 2026-11-10

This is a source review for the canonical Exercise Library owner (ALL-258), not an exercise catalog and not a prescription guide. It records which claims current reviews can support and where they cannot be generalized.

## Evidence rules for Coach

1. Bind anatomy, movement demands, cues and exercise benefits to the exact canonical **variation**. A goblet squat, barbell back squat, box squat and leg press are not interchangeable records.
2. Keep three levels of claim separate:
   - **General adaptation:** resistance training can develop a capacity such as maximal strength, muscle size or power in the studied population.
   - **Direct sport outcome:** a study measured a sport-specific performance outcome in a defined athlete population.
   - **Mechanistic rationale:** a movement or muscle action plausibly trains a demand. This is not proof of improved race performance.
3. Every claim must name its population, evidence tier, directness, linked source and limitations. A review of healthy adults must not be relabeled as proof of a sport-specific benefit.
4. Never convert a movement's muscle emphasis into an injury-prevention guarantee, an automatic performance prediction, or a claim that one exercise is mandatory.
5. Exercise suitability also depends on the athlete's goal, training age, phase, recent workload, next key session, confirmed equipment, accepted exercise restrictions and response to prior training. Evidence alone does not establish individual suitability.
6. When direct evidence is absent or uncertain, Coach may explain the general capacity being trained and label the sport transfer as indirect/uncertain. It should not invent a precise percentage benefit or claim causal certainty.

## Reviewed source findings

### General resistance training — healthy adults

**Currier et al., 2026, ACSM Position Stand / overview of reviews.** The overview synthesized 137 systematic reviews and more than 30,000 participants. Resistance training improved a broad set of strength, size, power and physical-function outcomes versus control. The reported prescription associations differ by target: heavier loads and full range of motion for strength; higher weekly volume for hypertrophy; moderate loads and fast concentric intent for power. Several popular programming variables did not consistently change outcomes across the reviewed literature.

**Allowed use:** broad general-adaptation evidence for healthy adults, with the actual outcome and training context stated.

**Not allowed:** using these population-level findings to claim that a particular exercise is best for runners, swimmers or cyclists; applying adult healthy-population advice to a person with a medical restriction as individualized clinical direction.

Source: [PubMed 41843416](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41843416/) · [Full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12965823/)

### Middle- and long-distance running

**Systematic review and meta-analysis, 2024.** This review examined strength-training methods and running economy in middle- and long-distance runners. Its reported conclusions indicate that high-load strength training, plyometric training and combined methods may improve running economy. Findings differ by method and test speed; the reviewed abstract reports potentially different patterns for plyometric and high-load approaches.

**Allowed use:** a bounded, population-specific direct outcome claim for running economy, provided the selected method, population and testing-speed limits are retained.

**Not allowed:** claiming every strength movement improves running economy; promising faster race times or injury prevention; generalizing the result to all trail-ultra demands, all experience levels or every running speed.

Source: [Systematic review and meta-analysis (2024)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11052887/) · [PubMed 38165636](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38165636/)

### Endurance cycling

**Heavy strength training systematic review and meta-analysis (published online 2025; journal issue 2026).** Seventeen studies with 262 cyclists were included. Heavy strength training showed statistically significant effects on cycling performance, cycling efficiency and anaerobic power in the pooled analysis, but not on several aerobic measures (including VO2max and maximal aerobic power). The authors characterize the certainty as low, so robust universal prescription claims are not justified.

**Allowed use:** evidence that heavy resistance training may support selected performance determinants in studied endurance cyclists; retain low-certainty and outcome-specific caveats.

**Not allowed:** saying strength training raises VO2max, that the same intervention suits every cyclist, or that a particular lift is proven superior from these pooled findings.

Source: [PubMed 40632222](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40632222/) · [Full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12881108/)

### Competitive front-crawl swimming

**Systematic review and meta-analysis (2024).** Thirteen studies with 267 competitive swimmers were included. The analysis found improvements in upper-limb maximal strength and some short- to middle-distance front-crawl outcomes. The authors report that methodology mattered; evidence quality for several distance/technical outcomes was low or very low, and findings should not be generalized to every stroke, event distance, recreational swimmer or shoulder condition.

**Allowed use:** narrowly framed claims for competitive front-crawl swimmers and the interventions/outcomes actually studied.

**Not allowed:** blanket prescriptions for all swimming, claims that a specific shoulder exercise prevents injury, or extrapolation to other strokes or long-distance open-water performance without supporting evidence.

Source: [PubMed 39129754](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39129754/) · [Full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11310150/)



### Middle- and long-distance running — race performance is not the same outcome as running economy

**Llanos-Lagos et al., 2024, systematic review and meta-analysis.** This is a separate review from the running-economy review above. It evaluated time-trial and time-to-exhaustion performance plus performance determinants in middle- and long-distance runners. It included 38 studies and 894 runners. High-load resistance training and combined methods showed beneficial effects on running performance; plyometric-only training did not reach statistical significance in this analysis. The authors graded certainty from very low to moderate. None of the methods significantly improved VO2max, velocity at VO2max, maximal metabolic steady state or sprint capacity in the pooled results.

**Allowed use:** a cautious, population-bounded claim that high-load or combined strength methods may improve measured running performance in middle-/long-distance runners. Keep the low-to-moderate certainty and intervention context attached.

**Not allowed:** merging this result with running-economy findings as if they are the same endpoint; stating that plyometrics are proven to improve race times based on this review; promising a faster ultra or trail-race result; assuming the reviewed intervention identifies a single best lift or a universal weekly dose.

Source: [PubMed 38627351](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38627351/) · [Full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11258194/) · DOI: 10.1007/s40279-024-02018-z

### Competitive swimming — multi-modal training evidence, not proof for an individual lift

**Wang et al., 2025, two-tier network meta-analysis.** This review compared training modalities for swimming performance in competitive swimmers. It included 36 randomized controlled trials and 844 participants. The network rankings favored combined aquatic and dryland approaches across several outcomes, with modality-specific rankings varying by distance and outcome; dryland resistance, core and power approaches showed selective rather than universal effects.

**Allowed use:** carefully qualified evidence that combined in-water and dryland preparation may benefit some measured competitive-swimming outcomes in the studied populations. A specific claim must preserve the stroke/distance/intervention/outcome actually represented by the evidence.

**Not allowed:** assigning a swimmer an arbitrary upper-body lift and claiming it improves their stroke; generalizing network rankings to recreational, masters, open-water or every-stroke athletes; treating indirect network comparisons as proof of one movement's superiority; making injury-prevention claims from performance outcomes.

Source: [PubMed 40951636](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40951636/) · [Full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12423047/) · DOI: 10.3389/fphys.2025.1636595

### Cycling — distinguish pooled intervention evidence from practical narrative guidance

**De Pablos et al., 2026, narrative review.** This review discusses resistance training and on-bike torque training for cyclists and offers practical programming suggestions. Importantly, its own methods describe a **non-systematic** search and the authors acknowledge that the selection strategy is prone to selection bias. Recommendations in this review should therefore not be stored as if they were a systematic-review result or a high-certainty universal prescription.

**Allowed use:** a lower-tier practical reference to inform hypotheses and questions for later athlete-specific evaluation, clearly labeled as narrative guidance and checked against the stronger cyclist-specific systematic review/meta-analysis above.

**Not allowed:** promoting its suggested frequency, intensity, exercise count or velocity-loss values to mandatory Coach rules; presenting these recommendations as causal proof; ignoring an athlete's race phase, next key session, recovery, equipment, lifting experience or current strength plan.

Source: [PubMed 42828469](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42828469/) · [Full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13633594/) · DOI: 10.1111/sms.70377

## Evidence interpretation update — practical consequences for Coach

- The two runner reviews answer different questions. Running economy is a physiological efficiency outcome; time-trial/time-to-exhaustion performance is a performance outcome. They must be represented as separate claims with separate sources and certainty.
- A sport-level intervention result does not prove that every exercise in that category transfers equally. The canonical movement variation still needs its own anatomy, joint-demand, equipment, coaching and caution evidence.
- Network meta-analysis rankings and narrative-review programming suggestions are not substitutes for direct exercise-variation evidence. Coach should describe the relevant training method and uncertainty, not infer a winning lift.
- No source reviewed here supports automatic injury-prevention promises, spot-reduction claims, or a guaranteed personal performance gain.



### Exact variation comparison — standing versus seated calf raise

**Kinoshita et al., 2023, within-subject controlled intervention.** Fourteen healthy, untrained adults completed a 12-week unilateral machine calf-raise program, twice weekly, with one leg trained in a standing/knee-extended position and the other in a seated/knee-flexed position. MRI measured muscle volume. Standing training produced larger medial and lateral gastrocnemius volume increases and larger whole triceps-surae gain; soleus growth was similar between conditions. The study used a small cohort and a supervised machine protocol.

**Allowed use:** a narrow exact-variation hypertrophy comparison for the studied untrained-adult population. It supports keeping standing and seated calf-raise as distinct movement variations and not describing seated raises as a proven soleus-isolation exercise.

**Not allowed:** claiming a running-economy, race-performance, tendon-stiffness, injury-prevention or fall-prevention benefit from this study; claiming that standing calf raise is best for every athlete; extrapolating the result directly to trained endurance athletes or other machines/protocols.

Source: [Frontiers full text](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2023.1272106/full) · [PubMed 38156065](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38156065/) · DOI: 10.3389/fphys.2023.1272106

### Exact variation comparison — Romanian, step-Romanian and stiff-leg deadlift

**Coratella et al., 2022, acute electromyographic comparison.** Ten experienced competitive bodybuilders performed Romanian, step-Romanian and stiff-leg deadlift variations at 80% 1RM. EMG measured selected posterior-chain muscles. During the ascending phase, the step-Romanian variation showed greater excitation in gluteus maximus, semitendinosus and longissimus than the other tested variations; the Romanian deadlift showed greater semitendinosus excitation than the stiff-leg variation; the stiff-leg variation showed greater gluteus-maximus excitation than the Romanian deadlift. The study reported fewer differences during the descending phase.

**Allowed use:** low-confidence mechanistic/exercise-emphasis context for distinguishing the exact variations and documenting which muscles were measured in this small, experienced-bodybuilder sample.

**Not allowed:** treating acute EMG as proof of superior hypertrophy, maximal strength, rehabilitation, running/cycling performance or injury prevention; assuming the same relative muscle response for beginners, other load/tempo/range protocols or every athlete; turning these results into an automatic recommendation.

Source: [PubMed 35162922](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35162922/) · [Full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8835508/) · DOI: 10.3390/ijerph19031903

### Exact variation comparison — seated versus prone leg curl

**Maeo et al., 2021, within-subject controlled intervention.** Twenty healthy adults trained one leg with a seated leg curl and the other with a prone leg curl at 70% condition-specific 1RM, 10 repetitions per set, five sets per session, twice weekly for 12 weeks. MRI measured individual and whole-hamstring volume. Whole-hamstring volume increased 14% with seated training versus 9% with prone training. The biarticular hamstrings grew more in the seated condition; the monoarticular hamstring response was similar. A separate eccentric-exercise test found similar muscle-damage protection after the two training conditions.

**Allowed use:** a narrow variation-level comparison for hamstring hypertrophy in the studied healthy adults; it supports preserving seated and prone curl as separate exercise identities and explaining the hip-position/muscle-length difference.

**Not allowed:** claiming the seated curl improves running speed, sprinting, injury prevention or rehabilitation outcomes; claiming prone curls are ineffective; generalizing the magnitude to trained athletes or every machine and training protocol.

Source: [PubMed 33009197](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33009197/) · [Full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7969179/) · DOI: 10.1249/MSS.0000000000002523

### Exact variation comparison — overhead versus neutral-arm cable elbow extension

**Maeo et al., 2023, within-subject controlled intervention.** Twenty-one adults trained one arm in an overhead cable-elbow-extension position and the other in a neutral-arm position. Both conditions used 70% condition-specific 1RM, 10 repetitions per set, five sets per session, twice weekly for 12 weeks, with load increased as tolerated. MRI-measured whole-triceps volume increased 19.9% in the overhead condition versus 13.9% in the neutral condition; long-head volume increased 28.5% versus 19.6%. The overhead condition achieved these increases despite lower absolute training loads.

**Allowed use:** a bounded variation-level comparison for triceps hypertrophy in the studied adult sample, with the shoulder position and intervention details retained. It supports separate overhead and neutral-arm variation identities.

**Not allowed:** claiming the overhead position improves swimming, throwing, cycling or general sport performance; promising injury prevention; assuming the overhead position is suitable for every shoulder or user; treating neutral-arm extensions as ineffective.

Source: [PubMed 35819335](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35819335/) · [Full text](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1080/17461391.2022.2100279) · DOI: 10.1080/17461391.2022.2100279

## Sport and athlete gaps still requiring research

The sources above do **not** establish exercise-to-performance mappings for every population ALL IN serves. Dedicated reviews and evidence grading are still needed for trail/ultra running, hiking, rucking, triathlon/multisport scheduling, rowing, beginners and older athletes with varied capability, strength/power sports, and athlete-specific restrictions. Do not fill those gaps with plausible-sounding muscle-transfer assumptions.

## Implementation mapping to ALL-258

The src/features/strength/exerciseScienceProfile.ts contract is intended to store evidence at the variation level:
- stable movement and variation IDs;
- movement pattern, laterality, contraction mode, skill and confirmed equipment IDs;
- sourced primary/secondary/stabilizer muscle roles and joint demands;
- caution flags and variation-specific written cues;
- progression, regression and alternative variation references;
- source records plus separate claims for muscle emphasis, general adaptation, direct sport transfer, load/recovery and caution;
- population, target sport/goal, directness, evidence tier, confidence and explicit limitations.

A profile may exist as a draft, but it is not Coach-eligible until the library owner marks it reviewed and it contains linked sources and claims. No exercise catalog rows are seeded by this research note.

## Next research and acceptance work

1. Build a variation-by-variation source review for common lifts and movement patterns; identify exactly which statements are anatomical description, general adaptation, direct sport outcome or mechanistic rationale.
2. Add athlete-context suitability rules only after the canonical profile fields and restriction/equipment owners are available. Missing equipment, unknown restrictions, pain context and incompatible next-session load must not be silently ignored.
3. Evaluate explanations across running, cycling, swimming, strength sport, beginner/walking, hiking/rucking and mixed-sport athletes. Include negative cases where an exercise is popular but unsupported or poorly timed for the athlete.
4. Coach must explain **why this variation is relevant now**, its limits, what it stresses and the alternative considered. It must not flatten every athlete into a generic leg/core routine.
